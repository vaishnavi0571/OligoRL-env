Reward function design decisions you still need to make
Your doc is theoretically rigorous, but several critical design choices are left open that will determine whether your environment actually works during the hackathon demo.
On the IHS profit component (40%)
The IHS handles the sign problem beautifully, but you haven't fixed the scale of the input. asinh(1,000,000) and asinh(1,000,000,000) are only ~4 units apart — which is the point — but this means the normalization divisor you choose will dominate everything. You need to decide: do you normalize by revenue at each step (dynamic), by a fixed max_expected_revenue constant (static), or by a rolling average? Dynamic is more adaptive but unstable early in episodes; static requires you to pre-tune a number.
Also: your amortization schedule says "amortized over time" but doesn't define the schedule length. 6 steps? 12? This directly changes whether heavy investment in step 1 looks like a rational long-term play or instant suicide in the reward signal.
On the solvency sigmoid (20%)
The 18-month target is borrowed from VC models but needs calibration to your timestep length. If one timestep = one week in simulation time, an 18-month target = runway of ~78 steps, which is enormous for a 30-step Easy task. You might want your ideal runway to be task-length-relative, not a fixed constant. Otherwise on Easy the sigmoid will almost never reach its inflection point.
On the GDP delta (30%)
The price index P_t is the most dangerous piece of your whole design. If it's just the average of all set_price actions, a single monopolist agent can collapse it by undercutting — that's a reward hack through price floor exploitation. You need to decide: is the price index agent-controlled (dangerous), exogenous with a slow drift (safer), or a weighted basket with inertia? This deserves explicit design, not just a variable name.
On the Gini penalty (−10%)
You describe exponential growth above the threshold but don't specify the exponent. This is not a minor detail — excess^2 vs excess^4 creates radically different behavior. With ^2 an agent will treat Gini as a light tax. With ^4 it will dominate everything else past Gini=0.7. You also need to define: is "wealth" measured as firm_cash only, or firm_cash + capital_stock? Including capital makes the metric more realistic but much harder to compute transparently for the LLM.
On difficulty weight presets
Your doc says Easy boosts the solvency weight, but doesn't say whether the other weights are renormalized to sum to 1.0, or whether the total reward magnitude changes. If you just set w_solvency=0.7 without renormalizing, your Easy reward max becomes ~1.1 instead of ~1.0, which will confuse the grader's normalization. Decide: always renormalize to sum to exactly 1.0, or keep the −0.1 Gini fixed and renormalize only the positive weights.

Decision 1: IHS normalization (profit component)
Use dynamic normalization by a rolling average of revenue, with a cold-start floor.
Don't use static max revenue — you can't know the right number before running the sim, and if you guess wrong the entire reward signal is either saturated or nearly zero from step 1. Don't use raw per-step revenue either — it causes wild swings when revenue spikes or collapses in a single step.
The formula: maintain a running exponential moving average of revenue with decay α=0.9. Use max(ema_revenue, floor) as the divisor, where floor is a small constant like 500.0 (whatever your sim's minimum meaningful transaction is). This gives you stability after the first few steps while still adapting to the agent's scale of play.
For amortization, use 10 steps fixed. Here's why: your Easy task is 30 steps, your Hard task will likely run longer. At 10 steps, an investment made in step 1 costs roughly 10% of its value per step — painful but not crippling, and fully paid off before the episode ends. 6 steps is too aggressive (punishes investment too hard), 12+ steps means the agent never sees the full payoff within a 30-step episode.

Decision 2: Solvency sigmoid target
Make the runway target task-relative, not a fixed constant.
Set ideal_runway = 0.5 × episode_length in timestep units, not months. On Easy (30 steps), that's 15 steps of runway — hitting the sigmoid's sweet spot right in the middle of the task. On Hard (say 60 steps), it scales to 30. This is cleaner than unit-converting "months" into steps, which requires you to define what a timestep represents in wall-clock time — an unnecessary complexity.
Keep the steepness k=0.2 but apply it to steps-of-runway, not months. The danger zone (steep gradient, near-vertical) will naturally sit in the 5–20 step range, which is exactly where you want the agent sweating.

Decision 3: Price index mechanism
Use an exogenous basket with inertia — the agent cannot directly control it.
This is the clearest decision of the five. An agent-controlled price index is a direct reward hack waiting to happen, and you already documented this risk in your doc. Here's the concrete design:
The price index P_t is updated by the environment each step according to:
P_t = 0.85 × P_{t-1} + 0.15 × market_avg_price_t
Where market_avg_price_t is the sales-weighted average of all realized transaction prices (not set prices — only prices that actually cleared demand), averaged across all agents including NPCs. The 0.85/0.15 inertia means even a monopolist who doubles their price today only moves the index by 15% of that change this step. It takes ~10 steps for a price shock to fully propagate — which is long enough for the GDP delta to register the real-GDP penalty before the agent can profit from it.
This also means the price index is observable and explainable in the semantic feedback string, which is important for your LLM agent.

Decision 4: Gini penalty exponent and wealth definition
Use exponent 3, and define wealth as firm_cash only.
Exponent 3 is the right middle ground. At ^2 the penalty barely registers — an agent running Gini=0.7 only pays (0.3/0.6)^2 = 0.25 in penalty, which the profit component easily absorbs. At ^4 the penalty becomes numerically explosive past Gini=0.8 and creates training instability. At ^3, an agent at Gini=0.7 pays ~0.42 — significant enough to force lobbying behavior, but not so large it swamps everything else.
For wealth, use firm_cash only. Here's the practical reason: capital stock is an internal simulation variable you'd have to carefully define, track, and expose in the observation space. Adding it to Gini calculation means the LLM agent now has to reason about an asset it can't directly observe or liquidate. Using just firm_cash keeps the Gini calculation fully transparent — the LLM can see everyone's cash in the state and verify the inequality score itself. Interpretability over realism here.
One addition worth making: floor each agent's wealth at 0 before computing Gini. Negative cash (debt) can cause the discrete Gini formula to return values outside [0,1], breaking your penalty scaling entirely.

Decision 5: Difficulty weight normalization
Always renormalize so positive weights sum to exactly 1 + 0.1 = 1.0 net — meaning the positive weights always sum to 0.9 and Gini stays fixed at −0.1.
Here's the rule: define each preset as raw unnormalized weights, then normalize the three positive components to sum to 0.9 before use. Gini is always −0.1, never touched. This means your reward is always bounded in [−0.1, 0.9] regardless of difficulty, and your grader never sees a different scale between presets.
