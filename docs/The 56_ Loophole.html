OligoRL · a small RL experiment

My RL agent beat my best bot by 56%. Then I turned one rule off.

It turned out the agent had read my rulebook better than I had.

Vaishnavi Yadav · 6 min read

I built OligoRL, a small simulated market. Five firms sell the same product, set their own prices and wages, and compete for a pool of 30 workers over 30-step games. I wrote eight scripted agents to act as baselines, trained a PPO agent to run one of the firms, and waited to see who won.

In the first serious run, PPO won by a lot. On validation games it finished with $106,877 in net worth. The best hand-written agent, a pricer I had named Premium pricer, finished with $68,499. That is 56% more. If you have trained RL agents before, you know what a number like that should make you feel. Suspicious.

So I ran the least glamorous experiment I could think of. I took the trained agent, changed nothing about it, and switched off one rule in the simulator. You can do the same below.

The trained agent is identical in both states. Both bars move because the hand-written agent was benefiting from the rule too. PPO just benefited far more.

What the rule does

The rule is called demand spillover. When a cheap firm sells out, some customers get turned away. Spillover passes a share of them on to firms that still have spare capacity. It seemed realistic to me: if one shop has run out of stock, you try the next one. In my first rules, 40% of turned-away customers were passed on.

Here is what I did not see coming. In those rules the rival firms never grew past five workers, and total demand was far above what the whole economy could produce. The rivals were routinely sold out, and a lot of customers were being turned away.

PPO worked out where they ended up. It charged about 22% more than its rivals and kept spare workers on the payroll, so it was always the firm with room left. It was not winning customers with a better price or smarter wages. It was collecting leftovers from stores that had run out.

It was not being clever about the market. It was being clever about my rulebook.

How much of the lead was the rule?

To measure it, I retrained PPO with spillover at 0%, 10% and 20% on the first rules. The 40% bar comes from the original pilot, which was a separate run, so read the chart as a shape and not as a precise curve. Tap a bar for the details.

More spillover, bigger lead. By this rough comparison, about four-fifths of the original lead came from the rule.

But look at the left bar. With spillover at zero, a PPO agent trained from scratch still beat the hand-written agents, by $7.0k, and it won on every validation seed. So my own headline would have been a little too dramatic. The agent that learned with the rule on did fall apart without it, and it ended $17,024 behind. An agent that learned without the rule kept a smaller lead that was real.

I fixed it. It found it again.

The agent was a better tester than my test agents

I had written the eight scripted agents on purpose. Some of them were built to attack the rules: one pays the maximum wage, one poaches workers from rivals, one was written specifically to probe spillover. None of them showed the problem clearly. The spillover probe, the Premium pricer, simply looked like a strong baseline. It took a learning agent, and its habit of pushing every lever as far as it will go, to show where that strength came from.

None of this is new in spirit. Machine learning people call it reward hacking or specification gaming, and a classic reference is Concrete Problems in AI Safety. What I can offer is a small, concrete, reproducible case, and a habit worth borrowing: treat the learning agent as a tester of your environment, not only as a competitor.

A side quest: the hire button

PPO also picked up a habit of requesting a hire on every single step. Against the default rivals that is free, because there is nobody unemployed to hire. Against rivals it had not seen, some overpay and go bankrupt, which pushes their workers into the job pool. PPO hired all of them and then went bankrupt itself in 47% of games.

In the final runs it still presses hire during the last three steps in nearly every game. That costs about 0.3%. I reported it and left it alone.

Where it ended up

After the fixes I wrote the test plan down before looking at any test game, trained five independent runs, and tested the median run once on 200 unseen games. Nothing was retrained or re-selected after testing.

What this does and does not show

It does show

  • A learning agent is a cheap, tireless tester of a simulator's rules. It found the spillover loophole in one pilot and again after my first fix, while eight hand-written agents had not shown it clearly.
  • A 56% lead can be mostly a property of the rulebook, and one switch is enough to find out.
  • After the fixes a real edge remains on familiar rivals: the median run won by 14%, all five runs beat the benchmark, and it won 92% of paired games with no bankruptcies, under a test plan written down in advance.
  • The setup is reproducible. Games are seeded, and stored games reproduce earlier results exactly.

It does not show

  • Anything about real markets. This is five firms and 30 workers.
  • That PPO copes with new opponents. Against unfamiliar rivals the median run's edge was not significant, and two of five runs went bankrupt too often.
  • Why the winning strategy works. The audit shows what it does, not why that pays. Turning spillover off also links firms less through demand than in a real market.

The next step I would take is to look for a spillover-style rule that links firms together without opening a loophole, and let PPO probe it again.

OligoRL has a browser dashboard where you can play a firm yourself, eight scripted agents, an automatic test suite and the PPO training code. It runs locally. The code is on GitHub.
